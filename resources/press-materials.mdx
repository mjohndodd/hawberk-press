A press kit is the professional backbone of your book promotion. It gives reviewers, podcasters, bloggers, and journalists everything they need to cover your work without chasing you for information — and a well-prepared kit signals that you take your book and your readers seriously. Hawberk Press prepares a press kit for every title we publish, and this guide walks you through what's in it, where to find it, and how to use it effectively.

## What's in Your Press Kit

Your Hawberk Press press kit is a curated set of materials covering the essentials that any media contact will want before deciding whether to cover your book.

If you need a press kit in a different format — for example, a plain-text version for accessibility, or a Word document you can edit directly — contact your editorial contact and they will prepare one for you.

## How to Use Your Press Materials

Press materials are most effective when you send them to the right people at the right time — and when you don't overwhelm recipients with more than they asked for.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify the right outlets for your book">
    Before sending anything, think about where your readers actually discover books. For dark speculative fiction, strong options include genre review sites, horror and weird fiction podcasts, SFF book clubs, and literary magazines with speculative leanings. A targeted list of twenty outlets is far more effective than a scattershot blast to two hundred.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Lead with a short, direct pitch email">
    Don't attach your entire press kit in a cold outreach email. Instead, write two or three sentences pitching your book, mention that you have a press kit available, and ask whether they'd like to receive a copy or a review file. Attach only the cover image.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Send the full press kit when requested">
    Once a contact expresses interest, reply promptly with the full press kit PDF, the relevant cover image size, and a link to a review copy (via NetGalley, BookFunnel, or direct from your editorial contact). Keep your email concise — let the kit speak for itself.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Follow up once, briefly">
    If you don't receive a response within two to three weeks, a single polite follow-up is appropriate. Don't chase beyond that — reviewers and editors are busy, and repeated contact is counterproductive.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Tailoring Your Materials for Specific Outlets

Your press kit provides a strong foundation, but a pitch that feels personalised to the outlet always performs better than a copy-paste. Here's how to adapt your approach depending on where you're pitching.

**Genre publications and specialist review sites**
Lead with the specific subgenre and the reading experience. Name comp titles your audience will recognise. If your book has a King in Yellow aesthetic, a Ligottian dread, or touches of folk horror, say so directly — specialist readers appreciate precision.

**General interest press and lifestyle publications**
Lead with the human story — yours or the book's. What does this book mean to readers who might not think of themselves as horror fans? Theme, emotional resonance, and the experience of reading tend to land better than genre labels here.

**Podcasts and audio interviews**
Pitch the conversation, not just the book. What can you talk about for twenty minutes? What angles — creative process, research, the cultural moment, the tradition you're writing in — would make for good listening? Mention these explicitly in your outreach.

**Local and regional press**
Local newspapers, magazines, and radio programmes often love a local-author angle. Lead with your connection to the area, then introduce the book. Keep the pitch short and the hook immediate.

<Tip>
  Keep a simple record of every outlet you contact — the date, the contact name, what you sent, and any response. It saves duplication, helps you follow up appropriately, and gives you a record to build on for your next book.
</Tip>

## Sample Pitch Angles

Different readers come to dark speculative fiction by different routes. Use these angles to shape how you frame your book depending on who you're pitching to.

<Accordion title="For dark speculative fiction readers">
  Lead with the reading experience and the tradition your book belongs to. Something like: *"[Title] is a work of dark speculative fiction in the tradition of [comp titles] — a story about [core premise] that lingers long after the last page. If your readers enjoy fiction that unsettles as much as it absorbs, I think [Title] will resonate."*

  Emphasise atmosphere, dread, ambiguity, and the sense that something is genuinely wrong with the world of the story. This audience responds to texture and tone.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="For readers drawn to the King in Yellow / Robert W. Chambers aesthetic">
  This is a dedicated and knowledgeable readership. Acknowledge the lineage directly — don't shy away from naming Chambers, the Yellow Sign, or the Carcosa tradition if your book engages with it. This audience appreciates authors who have done their homework and are contributing to a conversation, not just borrowing an aesthetic.

  A pitch might read: *"[Title] works within the Chambers tradition of cosmic dread and theatrical horror, offering [your specific angle or departure from the source material]."*
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="For readers of atmospheric and literary horror">
  Focus on prose quality, mood, and the kind of reading experience your book creates. Comp titles here might include authors known for literary style within the horror and weird fiction space. Mention if your book has been praised for its prose in any early reviews or blurbs.

  Avoid making this pitch too genre-heavy — readers of literary horror often come from a literary fiction background and respond to framing that foregrounds craft.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="For readers of atmospheric historical and period fiction">
  If your book has a historical setting or draws on period aesthetics, this angle opens doors with audiences who may not seek out horror explicitly. Lead with setting and historical atmosphere, then let the speculative or dark elements emerge as a flavour rather than the headline. Blogs covering Victorian fiction, early twentieth-century literature, or specific historical periods can be excellent allies.
</Accordion>
