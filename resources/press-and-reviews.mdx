Reviews are not vanity — they are infrastructure. On every major eBook retail platform, the number and quality of reader reviews directly affects how often your story is surfaced to new readers. A handful of thoughtful reviews from genre-appropriate outlets can open your work to communities you would never otherwise reach. This guide walks you through finding the right reviewers, approaching them well, and handling whatever they say.

## Why Reviews Matter

Retail algorithms on platforms like Amazon and Kobo use review volume and recency as signals of relevance. A story with even five genuine reviews will consistently outperform an equivalent story with none. Beyond the algorithmic benefit, reviews give undecided readers social proof — evidence that other humans have read your work and found it worth their time. For dark speculative fiction, where readers are especially attentive to genre-fit, a review from a trusted voice in the weird fiction community carries particular weight.

## Types of Coverage

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Professional Reviews" icon="star">
    Genre magazines and review publications — print and digital — that cover speculative and horror fiction. Harder to land but highly credible. Lead times are long; approach these early.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Blogger Reviews" icon="pen-nib">
    Independent book bloggers are the backbone of dark fiction reviewing. Many have loyal, niche audiences who trust their taste. Often more accessible than professional outlets and faster to publish.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Podcast Features" icon="microphone">
    Genre fiction podcasts may feature author interviews, readings, or story discussions. A good podcast appearance can be more personally engaging than any written review.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reader Reviews" icon="users">
    Reviews from ordinary readers on retail platforms and Goodreads. These accumulate over time and are driven primarily by giving readers a good reason to leave one — such as thanking early readers and gently asking for their thoughts.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Finding the Right Reviewers

The most important thing about a review pitch is that it lands in front of someone who genuinely reads work like yours. A reviewer who focuses on cosy mysteries is not the right audience for a King in Yellow-inflected horror novella, however large their following.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read the dark fiction community">
    Spend time in the spaces where dark fiction readers and reviewers gather — genre subreddits, Twitter/Bluesky conversations, newsletters. Identify reviewers who are already enthusiastic about the kind of fiction you write. These are your best targets.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check review policies">
    Most book bloggers publish their submission and review policies on their site. Read them before you pitch. If a reviewer says they're closed to unsolicited submissions, respect it. If they specify preferred genres, confirm yours fits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Look at what they've already reviewed">
    Has this reviewer covered weird fiction, dark speculative work, or independent press publications before? Do they engage seriously with the prose? A reviewer who likes what you like is far more likely to give your story a fair and useful reading.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build a shortlist">
    Aim for ten to fifteen carefully chosen targets rather than fifty indiscriminate ones. Quality of match matters far more than volume of pitches sent.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## How to Pitch a Reviewer

A good pitch is short, honest, and personal. Reviewers receive a great deal of promotional copy; what cuts through is a message that sounds like a human being wrote it for them specifically.

**What to include:**
- A brief, genuine introduction — who you are, that you're published by Hawberk Press
- A one-paragraph description of the story that conveys its tone and themes without overselling
- Why you thought of this reviewer specifically — reference something they've reviewed or written that made you think of them
- The format and length of the work (eBook, word count or page count)
- An offer to send a review copy, without pressure or obligation

**What not to do:**
- Don't open with "I think you'll love this" — let them decide
- Don't include retail links in the initial pitch; it reads as transactional
- Don't attach the manuscript unsolicited
- Don't send a mass pitch with multiple reviewers CC'd or BCC'd
- Don't follow up within a week of sending — give it at least a fortnight

**Following up:**
A single polite follow-up after two weeks is acceptable if you haven't heard back. If there's still no response, let it go. Persistence beyond one follow-up damages your reputation in a community that is smaller and more interconnected than it appears.

<Note>
  Hawberk Press coordinates review outreach to certain major genre outlets and review publications centrally. Before you approach any high-profile outlet — genre magazines, major horror review sites, or well-known dark fiction podcasts — check with the editorial team to confirm they haven't already made contact. Duplicate pitches are awkward for everyone and can close doors that would otherwise be open to you.
</Note>

## Advance Review Copies (ARCs)

An ARC is a copy of your story provided to reviewers before or around publication, in exchange for an honest review. Hawberk Press manages a central ARC distribution for each publication, sending copies to a curated list of reviewers the press has existing relationships with.

You may also share ARCs independently. If a reviewer you've identified is not on the Hawberk Press list, you can offer them a copy directly — coordinate with the editorial team to obtain a distributable file in the appropriate format. Keep a note of who has received an ARC so you can follow up appropriately and thank reviewers when their coverage goes live.

## Handling Reviews

<Accordion title="Responding to positive reviews">
  A warm, brief acknowledgement is always appropriate — a public thank-you on social media, tagging the reviewer if they've posted publicly. Keep it gracious and genuine. Avoid effusive or sycophantic responses; they can make you look as though you've never received praise before. Share the review through your own channels, and through Hawberk Press's if the editorial team agrees it's worth amplifying.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Responding to mixed reviews">
  A mixed review — one that finds real merit alongside genuine criticism — is a useful document. Read it carefully and privately. There may be something instructive in it for your next project. Do not respond publicly to the critical elements, even if you disagree. Thank the reviewer for their time if you choose to respond at all, and leave it at that.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Responding to negative reviews">
  Don't. A negative review, left alone, affects almost no one. An author responding to a negative review — however measured the response — creates a far more memorable and damaging story. Readers and reviewers talk to each other. The author who publicly disputed a bad review will be remembered for that long after the review itself is forgotten. Read it once, close the tab, and write the next story.
</Accordion>

## Keeping Track

Once you're pitching more than a handful of reviewers, you need a simple system. A spreadsheet with the following columns is all you need:

| Reviewer / Outlet | Platform | Date Contacted | Format Sent | Response | Review Published | Link |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Update it as responses come in. Note which outlets Hawberk Press is handling centrally so you don't overlap. Review your list at the one-month and three-month marks to follow up where appropriate and retire contacts who haven't responded.

<Tip>
  Keep a separate tab for reviewers you'd like to approach for future projects — people who reviewed your current story positively, or whose taste you've observed over time. Building these relationships slowly is more valuable than any individual review.
</Tip>
