Editorial work at Hawberk Press is built on collaboration, not correction. The goal of every round of feedback is to help your story become the best version of itself — truer to your vision, sharper in its execution, more resonant for readers. Your editor brings a careful eye and a genuine enthusiasm for the work; you bring the story, its intent, and its voice. The process is a conversation, and your perspective matters at every stage.

## What Editorial Feedback Looks Like

Editorial feedback typically moves through three distinct modes, each addressing a different layer of the manuscript.

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Developmental Editing" icon="map">
    The first pass focuses on the story as a whole — its structure, pacing, character, atmosphere, and the overall logic of the narrative. Developmental notes might ask questions rather than prescribe answers: *Does this scene earn its length? Does the ending feel inevitable?* These are the big-picture conversations.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Line Editing" icon="pen-nib">
    Once the structure is settled, the focus narrows to the sentence and paragraph level. Line edits are concerned with clarity, rhythm, precision of language, and the consistency of tone and voice. This is where the prose is refined without being rewritten.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Copyediting" icon="spell-check">
    The final editorial pass catches technical issues — spelling, punctuation, grammar, consistency of names and details, and adherence to house style. Copyediting is not a creative intervention; it's a quality check before the manuscript moves to production.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

Not every story requires all three passes at equal depth. Your editor will tailor the process to what the work actually needs, and will be transparent with you about what kind of feedback is coming and why.

## How Many Rounds to Expect

Most short fiction at Hawberk Press moves through **one to two editorial rounds** before reaching the copyediting stage. The number of rounds depends on the manuscript — some stories arrive in near-final shape and need only light attention; others benefit from a deeper developmental conversation before the line work begins.

Your editor will give you a clear sense of the scope before the first round begins. If additional rounds are needed beyond the initial plan, they'll discuss that with you rather than simply sending more notes without context.

## How to Respond to Editorial Notes

Your editor will deliver feedback in a format that's easy to work through — typically a combination of inline comments and a cover letter or editorial memo summarising the key points. When you receive a round of notes:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read everything before you start revising">
    Take the full set of notes in before making any changes. Developmental feedback in particular benefits from being understood as a whole rather than addressed point by point in sequence. You may find that some concerns resolve naturally once you address a central issue.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sit with anything that surprises you">
    It's normal for some feedback to feel unexpected, or even to sting a little. Give yourself time before responding. A note that initially seems off-base often makes more sense after a day's distance, and your reply — whether to revise or push back — will be clearer for the pause.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Revise, then respond">
    Return your revised manuscript along with any notes or questions for your editor. You don't need to document every change, but flagging the significant decisions you've made — and anywhere you'd like further input — keeps the conversation moving efficiently.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  You don't have to accept every suggested change. Your editor's job is to serve the story, and so is yours. If a revision doesn't feel right, say so — briefly and specifically. The best editorial relationships are ones where both parties feel free to push back respectfully.
</Tip>

## If You Disagree With a Suggested Change

Editorial notes are suggestions, not mandates. You are always the author of record, and the final voice of the work is yours. If you disagree with a suggested change:

- **Say so directly.** A short explanation of your reasoning is all that's needed — *I want to keep this ambiguous because the uncertainty is the point* is a complete and valid response.
- **Propose an alternative.** If a note identifies a real problem but the proposed solution doesn't feel right, try offering your own fix. The concern may be legitimate even if the remedy isn't.
- **Escalate gracefully if needed.** In the rare case where you and your editor reach an impasse, the editorial lead is available to step in and help find a path forward. This process is designed to support the work, and disagreement handled well usually produces better results than either party simply deferring.

## The Author's Role in Final Proofing

Before your story moves to production, you'll receive a final proof of the formatted manuscript — the text as it will appear in the published eBook. Read this carefully. Your editor will have reviewed it too, but you know your work better than anyone, and a fresh pass from the author catches things no one else will.

<Note>
  At the proofing stage, please focus on genuine errors — typos, missing words, formatting anomalies. Major revisions to the text are not possible once the manuscript is in production layout. If you spot something significant, raise it with your editor immediately so it can be assessed before the production deadline.
</Note>
